Executive summary

REIT Runway is a capital-sourcing tool for commercial real estate. It reads the latest 10-K and 10-Q of 146 listed U.S. equity REITs from SEC EDGAR and finds the $157B of debt they have coming due by October 2028. For each company it measures how much of that it can pay from cash, retained cash flow and its credit line, prices the rest at today's rates, and ranks the REITs by how much capital they need from outside.

For capital providers

JV equity, preferred equity, mezzanine and private credit

Find REITs with a funding gap they can't close on their own. Each company shows its maturity schedule, the cash and credit it has, the shortfall, and whether its leverage and dividend coverage point to preferred or JV equity rather than another bond deal.

For bankers and lenders

Debt capital markets, bank lenders and debt advisors

See which REITs have to come to market in the next two years and what it will cost them. Every company is priced off today's 10-year Treasury by leverage, with the rate increase on its maturing debt, flags for thin coverage and pitch notes for the first call.

Cash, retained cash flow and credit lines cover most of what's due. The $41.6B they don't cover has to come from a bond deal, a new loan, preferred or JV equity, or asset sales, and each of those is a financing assignment.

The rate sheet

What maturing REIT debt costs today

Indicative all-in pricing as of Oct 26, 2026, built from a spread over the latest 10-year Treasury close and SOFR, which update every business day. The spread steps up with book leverage. In the tool you can change any of these numbers, and every REIT is re-sized.

Spreads are indicative assumptions, not quotes. REITs whose cash flow covers interest 3.5× or more are priced as investment grade whatever their book leverage. Refinancing everything due at these rates adds about $3.3B a year in interest across the group.

How it's built

From SEC filings to a call list

Every number comes from the companies' own SEC filings. Most of the work went into reading maturity schedules that each REIT reports a little differently, and checking every one against the balance sheet.

1

Pull the filings

The latest 10-K and 10-Q for 146 listed equity REITs from SEC EDGAR, checked every business day for new filings.

2

Read the schedule

Debt maturities by year from the XBRL tags, the debt-note table in the 10-K, or read by hand where neither parses.

3

Count the sources

Cash on the balance sheet, operating cash flow less dividends over the last 12 months, and undrawn revolver capacity where the filing tags it.

4

Price the refinance

What's due, repriced at today's 10-year plus a spread set by leverage, against the rate each company pays now.

5

Rank and flag

Sort each REIT into an outcome, score its capital need, and write pitch notes from its numbers.

Checked: every schedule

Each maturity schedule is added up and compared with the total debt on the balance sheet. Schedules that cover less than 90% are flagged.

Fixed: lease tables read as debt

Some 10-Ks print a lease-payment table beside the debt table, and the first pass picked up the wrong one for several REITs. The reader now tests every candidate table against reported debt and keeps the closest match.

Missed: 4 REITs

A few companies don't publish a maturity schedule the reader can use, so they're shown as insufficient data instead of guessed.
